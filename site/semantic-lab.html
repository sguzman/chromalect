Can small pictograms communicate meaning?

This compares a single emoji, a short pictogram combination, a written gloss, and no annotation. It is an illustrative interface experiment, not a source-verified dictionary, and it does not change the completed 1,000-character glyph coloring.

Important: Chinese characters can have multiple senses, and compound words often mean more than the sum of their characters. These prototypes annotate selected meanings, never the Hanzi strokes or radicals themselves. We do not assume a composition is valid just because its emoji look plausible.

What the first test can tell us

A single pictogram may help with concrete senses such as water, fire or mountains. A two- or three-symbol combination might help with actions or associations, but juxtaposition can also create a wrong meaning. Words such as 火车 (train) need their word-level meaning, not a picture of fire followed by a picture of a vehicle.

There is no demand for 100% emoji coverage. If a composition is ambiguous, the system should show a clear gloss or abstain. A future source-backed dataset will attach meaning annotations to reviewed senses and lexical entries, not blindly to every character shape.

Read the experiment and acceptance plan. All component identity, Pinyin and tone coloring remain separate and unchanged. The production semantic Direct/Marker mode remains unavailable until a sense inventory and accessible symbol annotations have passed independent review.
